Mobile notary fees: what to charge for travel and loan signings (the math)

Mobile notary pricing confuses people because it's really two prices stapled together: a notarization fee that state law caps, and a travel/convenience fee that is ordinary business pricing. The first number you look up. The second number is where mobile notaries underprice themselves — usually because they never put their real costs into a formula. This guide is the formula.

The two-part fee structure

Part 1 — the notarial act fee. Set by your state, typically $5–$15 per notarized signature, and it is a cap, not a suggestion — you can charge less, never more. Look yours up in your state's notary handbook (Secretary of State website) before quoting anything.

Part 2 — the travel fee. In most states this is separate from the capped act fee and priced by you; a few states cap or regulate it too (some set per-mile rates), and many require you to disclose it in advance and itemize it separately from the act fee. Two rules that keep you safe everywhere: quote the full total before the appointment, and show the act fee and travel fee as separate lines.

The travel-fee floor: a formula, not a feeling

Your travel fee has a floor below which the appointment loses money. Price the trip, not the stamp:

Floor = (round-trip miles × vehicle cost/mile) + (door-to-door hours × your target hourly rate)

Worked example — an appointment 12 miles away:

CostMathAmount
Vehicle24 mi round trip × $0.70/mi $16.80
Your time1.25 h door-to-door × $40/h target $50.00
Travel floor ~$67

Add two notarized signatures at a $10 state cap and the honest quote is about $87. Notice what the math just told you: the commonly quoted $25 travel fee only works for appointments a few minutes away. Most local mobile appointments genuinely cost the customer $50–$100 all-in — your quote landing there isn't gouging, it's arithmetic.

A tiered travel table (so you stop re-deriving it)

Bands beat exact-mile quotes for the same reason flat project pricing beats hourly estimates: the customer gets a fast, confident number and you stop negotiating pennies. If your state regulates travel fees, your bands live under that ceiling.

Loan signings: run the effective-hourly math before you celebrate

Loan signing packages pay a flat $75–$200 (signing services cluster at $90–$125; direct escrow work runs ~$150+). The flat fee looks great against a one-hour appointment — but the appointment is not the job:

Where the time goesHours
Print two document sets (~300 pages), prep0.50
Drive, both ways0.67
The signing itself1.00
Scan-backs, drop-off, status updates0.58
Total2.75

A $125 signing ÷ 2.75 hours = ~$45/hour effective, before paper, toner, E&O insurance, and the annual background screening. That's still decent money — but it's the real number to compare offers with, and it's why a $90 signing 40 minutes away can be worth declining. The same actual-hours-in-the-denominator logic applies to any freelance work — the effective hourly rate guide walks through it client by client.

Getting paid without chasing

General notary work is pay-at-the-door. Signing services and escrow clients pay on invoice — commonly 30–45 days later — so the moment you take signing-service work you have receivables: number every invoice, put a real due date on it, and chase on a schedule (the payment-reminder templates transfer directly). A simple tracker with one row per signing — who, date, fee, invoiced, paid — is enough; our Freelance Client & Invoice Tracker ($14) does exactly that in Excel, and the free rate calculator will gross up your income goal for taxes and non-billable hours to give you the target hourly rate the travel formula needs.

Business-pricing math only — not legal advice. Notarial act fees are capped by state law, travel-fee rules vary by state, and you are responsible for following your state's notary statutes and handbook. Verify your caps with your Secretary of State before setting prices.
