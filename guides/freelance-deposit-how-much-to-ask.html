Freelance deposits: how much to ask up front, and how to ask

A deposit isn't about distrust — it's the cheapest credit check that exists. A client who won't put anything down before you start is telling you, before the work begins, how the final invoice is going to go.

The going rates

  • 25–50% of the project price is the standard band across freelance design, writing, development, and marketing work. Most clients who hire freelancers regularly expect a number in this range.
  • New client, no history: lean 50%. The deposit is doing the most work exactly when you have the least evidence they'll pay.
  • Long-standing client you trust: 25–30% is usually enough — at that point it's cash-flow smoothing, not protection.
  • Small projects (under about $500): 100% up front. Chasing a $180 balance costs more than it's worth; price it as pay-then-start.
  • Front-loaded costs: if the project makes you buy things early — stock assets, subcontractors, travel — set the deposit to at least cover those, whatever percentage that works out to.

The exposure math

The percentage isn't a politeness setting — it's how much of the project you're personally financing. Take a $3,000 project and assume the worst case: the client disappears when you're halfway through the work.

DepositPaid up frontYour unpaid work at the halfway mark
0%$0$1,500
25%$750$750
50%$1,500$0
100%$3,000$0 (client carries the risk)

At 50%, a mid-project ghosting costs you nothing but the tail you hadn't started. At 0%, you've lent a stranger $1,500 interest-free with no paperwork. That's the whole argument, and it's why the band centers where it does.

How to ask without it being awkward

The trick is that you don't ask — you state it as how you work, in the quote, before anyone's emotionally invested. A line item beats a conversation:

Payment: 50% deposit to book the project (invoice sent on acceptance; work begins when it's paid), 50% on delivery.

If it comes up in conversation instead, the same content, said plainly:

I take a 50% deposit to get a project on the calendar — I'll send that invoice today, and I start as soon as it lands. The balance is due on delivery.

Two details do the heavy lifting: the deposit is tied to booking (it buys your calendar slot, which is true), and work begins when it's paid — stated up front, so a slow deposit delays the schedule instead of you.

Longer projects: deposit + milestones

Past roughly a month of work, one deposit stops being enough protection, because the unpaid middle grows. Split the price so no single unpaid chunk is bigger than you can afford to lose — a common shape is 40% to start, 30% at an agreed midpoint deliverable, 30% on delivery. Each milestone gets its own invoice, and the midpoint one follows the same rule as the deposit: the next phase starts when it's paid.

Deposits and your other payment terms

The deposit guards the front of the project; your invoice terms guard the back end. They're one system: deposit stated in the quote, then late-fee terms on the balance invoice, and a calm reminder sequence when a due date slips. The deposit invoice itself is a normal invoice — number, due date, payment instructions — so run it through the invoice checklist like any other.

If the client pushes back

Some large companies genuinely can't do deposits — procurement pays on delivered milestones only. That's a process constraint, not a red flag; answer it with a smaller first milestone ("first deliverable in week one, invoiced immediately") instead of dropping protection entirely. But an individual or small business that refuses any up-front payment while pressing you to start is the red flag the deposit exists to catch. Declining that project costs you the deposit you were never going to see anyway.
