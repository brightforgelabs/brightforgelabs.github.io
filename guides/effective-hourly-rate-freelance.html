Effective hourly rate: what you actually earn per hour (and per client)

Your quoted rate is what you charge. Your effective rate is what you actually earn: everything a client paid, divided by every hour the work really took — meetings, revisions, emails, and admin included. For most freelancers the two numbers are 20–40% apart, and the gap is invisible until you do the division. This guide does the division.

The formula (and the rule that makes it honest)

Effective hourly rate = total actually paid ÷ total actual hours. The rule that makes it honest: actual hours means all of them. The kickoff call, the fourth revision round, the "quick question" emails, invoicing and chasing the invoice — if the client's project consumed the hour, the hour counts. Billable hours measure what you invoiced; actual hours measure what the relationship cost you. Only the second number tells you what the work pays.

A worked example

You quote a $3,000 project at $150/hour, estimating 20 hours. What actually happens:

Where the time wentHours
The work you scoped21
Calls and meetings4
Revision rounds beyond the plan3
Email, invoicing, admin2
Total30

3,000 ÷ 30 = $100/hour effective — a third below the rate on the proposal, on a project that felt like it went fine. That's the typical case, not the horror story: freelancers routinely underestimate projects by 20–50%, and almost nobody bills the meetings.

The per-client audit (where it gets useful)

One project's effective rate is trivia. The same math run per client over the last 12 months is a business decision. Total what each client actually paid you, divide by the hours each actually consumed:

ClientPaid, last 12 moActual hours Effective rate
The "big" agency account$24,000340 $71/h
The quiet monthly retainer$14,400130 $111/h
One-off projects (combined)$6,00045 $133/h

This is the "big client" illusion: the account that feels most important — biggest invoices, most meetings, most mindshare — is often the worst payer per hour of your life it consumes. The quiet retainer you barely think about is the best business you have. You can't see that from revenue totals; you can only see it from effective rate.

Why the gap happens

  • Unbilled hours: meetings, calls, and email are real work that rarely makes the invoice.
  • Scope creep: extras absorbed without a change order are hours added to the denominator with nothing added to the numerator — the scope-creep guide covers how to charge for them instead.
  • Optimistic estimates: fixed prices built on best-case hours (the 20–50% underestimate) quietly discount your rate — see hourly vs day rate vs project pricing for when a flat price protects you and when it doesn't.
  • Revision-heavy clients: the same deliverable at the same price can cost double the hours depending on who's reviewing it.

What to do with a low number

First, know your floor: your target rate — the one that covers your income goal, taxes, and non-billable time — comes from the math in how to set your freelance rate (or run your numbers through the free Rate Calculator). Then compare each client's effective rate against it and apply a simple rule of thumb: a client sitting 30%+ below your target rate gets repriced at the next natural opening, or replaced when something better arrives. Repricing can mean a rate raise, a switch of pricing model, revision limits, or billing the meetings — whichever attacks that client's particular gap. A client you'd lose over any of those was being subsidized by your unpaid hours all along.

You can't audit what you didn't record

The whole exercise needs exactly two numbers per client: what they paid and roughly the hours they consumed. Payments you can reconstruct from invoices; hours you can't reconstruct at all — you have to be logging them as you go, even coarsely.
