Time blindness and ADHD: planner strategies that actually anchor your day

"I sat down at 10am, blinked, and it was 4pm." If time passes in a way you can't feel, willpower isn't the fix — external structure is. Here's how to build it with paper, a pen, and one timer.

Why time blindness beats to-do lists

A to-do list says what; it says nothing about when or how long. If your sense of elapsed time is unreliable, a list of ten items reads as "all equally doable today," and the day ends with two done and eight rolled over. Every strategy below does one job: it takes time out of your head and puts it somewhere you can see it.

1. Time anchors, not schedules

Full hour-by-hour schedules tend to collapse by 10:30, and then the whole plan feels dead. Anchors are lighter: pick two or three fixed points in the day (say 9:00, 13:00, 16:00) and attach one priority to each. The space between anchors stays flexible, so one overrun doesn't torch the plan. On a daily page, this is three lines, written next to real clock times — writing the time down matters more than hitting it exactly.

2. Plan backwards from the deadline

Forwards planning ("I'll start soon") relies on the very time-sense that's missing. Backwards planning starts at the fixed point and subtracts: out the door at 2:00, twenty minutes to drive, ten to get dressed and find keys, so the alarm is set for 1:25 — the alarm, not the intention. Do the subtraction on paper once, then let the timer carry it. For bigger deliverables, work backwards in days instead of minutes and write each step on its own daily page.

3. Use buffer math: estimate, then multiply

Most people underestimate task length; time blindness turns that from a rounding error into a planning failure. The fix is mechanical, not moral: estimate, then multiply by 1.5× for familiar tasks and 2× for anything new or dreaded. "One hour" becomes a 90-minute block. If you finish early, that's a bonus break — the plan survives either way. A planner page with room for estimated vs actual next to each block turns this into a feedback loop: after two weeks you'll know your personal multiplier instead of guessing.

4. Make time visible while you work

One analog clock in your line of sight and one cheap kitchen timer do most of what an app subscription promises. Set the timer when you start a block, and treat it going off as information ("that was an hour"), not a verdict. Hyperfocus is the flip side of time blindness — the timer is what lets you enjoy it without losing the afternoon.

5. Close the day with an "actually did" line

Time blindness also distorts the past: a day where you handled six real things can feel like "I did nothing." Two minutes at the end of the day writing what actually happened — next to what was planned — rebuilds an honest record. It's also the data that makes your buffer multiplier accurate.
