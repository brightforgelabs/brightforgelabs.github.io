Quote vs estimate vs invoice: which to send, and when

Three documents, one axis: commitment. An estimate is a prediction, a quote is an offer, an invoice is a request for money that's owed. Freelancers get burned when a document from one column gets read as one from another — a casual estimate the client heard as a fixed price is the classic version. Here's the clean split, and the wording that keeps each document in its lane.

The one-screen answer

DocumentWhen it's sentBinding? Its one job
EstimateEarly, scope still fuzzy No — it's a projection Give the client a realistic cost range so both sides can decide whether to keep talking
QuoteScope is defined Yes, once accepted, for the validity period you set Offer a fixed price for a fixed scope, with payment terms attached
InvoiceWork delivered (or a milestone reached)Yes — it's a payment obligation Collect a specific amount by a specific date

A proposal, for completeness, is a quote wrapped in context — the client's problem, your approach, then the quoted price and terms. When the client already knows what they want, a quote alone is enough; when they're choosing between approaches (or freelancers), send a proposal and put the quote inside it.

Estimates: useful precisely because they're loose

An estimate's honesty is a range: "roughly $1,800–$2,400 depending on how many page templates survive the design round." A single naked number gets remembered as a promise, and you'll negotiate against your own guess later. Two habits keep estimates safe:

  • Name what moves the number. One line: "the low end assumes content arrives written; the high end covers two revision rounds." Now a scope change moves the price without an argument.
  • Restate before you exceed. The professional norm is that a final bill lands within roughly 10–15% of the estimate unless the scope changed — and if you can see you'll blow past it, say so before the hours are spent, not on the invoice.

How to write a quote that holds up

A quote is the document you're committing to, so it's built, not guessed:

  1. Price from your real rate. Scope items × honest hours × a rate that covers costs, taxes, and non-billable time — the math is in how to set your freelance rate, or use the free Rate Calculator. Fixed price? Add the 10–20% risk premium the proposal guide covers.
  2. 4–7 line items. Fewer reads as a mystery number; more reads as a receipt from a hardware store. Each line a phase or deliverable the client recognizes, described as an outcome ("checkout flow, built and tested") rather than a technique.
  3. A validity date. "This quote is valid for 21 days." Without one, the price you offered in March is the price they accept in September — after your rates went up and your calendar filled. 14–30 days is standard.
  4. Payment terms, in the quote itself. The deposit (25–50% up front — and work starts when it clears), the milestone schedule on longer projects, your payment terms and late fee. Terms stated in an accepted quote were agreed to; terms first seen on an invoice are a negotiation.   5. One acceptance action. "Reply 'accepted' and I'll send the deposit invoice" — which is also the bridge to the next document.

From accepted quote to invoice

The handoff is mechanical, and that's the point:

  • The deposit invoice goes out the day the quote is accepted — acceptance is warmest on day one, and the project doesn't start until the deposit clears.
  • Invoice lines mirror quote lines. The client approved those words once; an invoice that matches gets paid without a "what's this line?" round-trip. (The full field-by-field list is in what to put on an invoice.)
  • Reference the quote on every invoice ("per quote #2026-014, accepted May 3") — each milestone invoice inherits the agreement instead of re-arguing it.
  • New scope gets a new quote. Mid-project requests become change orders — a mini-quote with its own price and acceptance — never a silent adjustment to the final invoice.

The failure modes, so you can dodge them

  • An estimate that reads like a quote. No range, no assumptions — the client heard a fixed price. Label the document "Estimate" and give a range.
  • A quote with no validity date — an open offer at last year's rate.
  • A quote silent on payment terms — you'll discover the client's "we pay net 60" policy on invoice day.
  • An invoice that doesn't match the quote — every mismatched line is a reason to pay you later.
