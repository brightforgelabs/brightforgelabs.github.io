Late fees on freelance invoices: how much, and how to word it

A late fee's job isn't revenue — it's to make your due date mean something. The number matters less than two other things: that it's in writing before the work, and that your invoices actually get flagged the day they go late.

The two models

Percentage interest — commonly 1.5–2% per month on the unpaid balance — is the norm for B2B freelance work. It scales with the invoice, so it stays meaningful on a $5,000 project and trivial on a $150 one.

Flat fee — typically $25–50 once the invoice passes due — fits smaller invoices, where a percentage would round to pocket change. A $30 flat fee on a $400 invoice gets noticed; 1.5% ($6) does not.

Two things jump out. First, on typical freelance invoice sizes the dollar amounts are modest — which is the point: the fee is a signal, not a profit center. Second, 1.5% per month is 18% annualized (2% is 24%), and some places cap the interest you may charge. Check the rules where you and your client are before picking a number.

The rule that outranks the number

A late fee added after an invoice is already overdue is a request, not a term. For it to stick, it has to appear before the work — in your contract or accepted proposal — and again on the invoice itself. Surprise fees don't get paid; they get argued with, and they burn the relationship you were trying to protect. The same before-the-work logic is why a deposit up front is the stronger half of the protection: the late fee guards the tail of the project, the deposit guards the start.

Grace periods and terms

Most freelancers who charge fees start them 5–15 days after the due date, not day one — the grace period absorbs payment-run timing and makes the fee feel fair when it does land. Pairing net-15 terms with a short grace period usually beats net-30 with none: the first reminder goes out sooner, while the fee stays a background incentive.

Paste-ready wording

Payment due within 15 days of invoice date. Balances more than 10 days past due accrue interest at 1.5% per month.
Payment due by [date]. A $35 late fee applies to balances unpaid 10 days after the due date.

One sentence in the payment-terms line of the invoice, same sentence in the contract. That's the whole implementation — see what else belongs on the invoice.

Should you actually collect it?

Many freelancers quietly waive the fee once payment lands — and that's fine. The policy still did its work: invoices with stated late terms signal that you notice lateness, and clients pay the noticers first. Enforce it for repeat offenders; waive it, explicitly and graciously, for a good client's one-off slip.
