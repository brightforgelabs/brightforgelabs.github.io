How to raise your freelance rates (the math, the timing, the email)

Most freelancers set a rate once, then quietly keep it for years while their costs and skills grow. The raise feels risky — "what if clients leave?" — but almost nobody runs the numbers on what leaving would actually cost. The math says a raise is far safer than it feels.

The break-even math: how many clients can walk?

Raise your rate by 15% and a client who stays pays 15% more per hour. That surplus buys you room: you stay at last year's income even if some work disappears. The break-even is simple — after a raise of r%, you can lose up to r ÷ (100 + r) of your billable hours before you earn less than before.

RaiseHours you can lose and still break even
10%9% of your hours
15%13% of your hours
20%17% of your hours
25%20% of your hours
30%23% of your hours

Worked example: you bill $60/hour for 1,000 hours a year — $60,000. Raise to $69 (+15%). Break-even is 60,000 ÷ 69 ≈ 870 hours: an entire small client (130 hours) can leave and you still match last year — while working three weeks less. Anything you re-sell at the new rate is pure gain. And in practice, a reasonable raise with proper notice rarely loses anyone at all.

How much to raise: pick your situation

SituationTypical raiseNotes
Routine annual adjustment, same service5–10% Small enough that clients absorb it without discussion; compounds meaningfully over the years.
Booked solid 4+ weeks out, turning work away 15–25%Demand is telling you the price is low. Excess demand is the cleanest raise signal there is.
Skills, results, or positioning upgraded since you set the rate20–30%You're selling a better product than the old price was set for.
Legacy client far below your current rateTwo stepsA 40% jump invites a hard no. Step to the midpoint now, the rest in 6 months — and say that plan out loud.
Changed niche or service modelRe-price from scratchDon't "raise" — re-derive. Run the rate formula against the new reality.

When (and to whom) to announce it

  1. New clients first — today. Quote the new rate to every new lead. Nobody negotiating a first project knows or cares what you charged last year. Two or three yeses at the new number is market proof it holds.
  2. Existing clients with 30+ days' notice. Tie the start date to a natural boundary — January 1, the new quarter, or the next project. Budget season (December–January) is the least surprising moment for a business to hear about a price change.
  3. Let current projects finish at the agreed price. Mid-project changes feel like a bait-and-switch and sour good relationships. The raise applies to what's booked next.

The email (paste-ready)

Subject: Rate update from [date]

Hi [name],

A quick heads-up on pricing: from [date, 30+ days out], my rate for [service] moves from [$old] to [$new] per [hour/day/project]. Everything currently booked stays at the agreed price, and nothing else about how we work changes.

I've really enjoyed the work on [current/recent project] and I'm looking forward to more of it this year.

[Your name]

What makes it work: a date, a number, no apology, and no essay. The raise is stated as settled fact, not floated as a question — "would it be okay if…" invites a negotiation you don't want.

If a client pushes back

"I understand — budgets are real. The new rate is where my pricing is now, but if it helps, we can trim the monthly scope to [reduced deliverable] to stay near your current spend."

Adjust scope, never the rate: a discount teaches clients that your prices are opening bids. Most pushback is a reflex, not an exit — a client who values the work rarely re-opens vendor search over a reasonable increase. If someone does leave over 15%, the break-even table above shows how little that usually costs; the hours they release get re-sold at the new rate. One caution: don't raise on a client whose invoices are already overdue — collect first, using a calm reminder sequence, then re-price.

Common mistakes

  • Apologizing. "I'm so sorry, I hate to do this" reframes a normal business event as a favor to forgive.
  • Over-explaining. One reason is plenty. Three reasons read as three things to argue with.
  • Negotiating against yourself. Announcing the new rate and immediately offering the old one "for you" undoes the raise before the client even replies.
  • Raising blind. If you don't know which clients are below your current rate, that's a bookkeeping gap, not a pricing one — see the next section.
