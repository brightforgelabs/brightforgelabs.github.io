Debt snowball vs avalanche: the spreadsheet math

Two famous payoff strategies, one question: which order do you attack your debts in? The honest answer from the math: the difference is usually smaller than the internet argues about — and the method you'll actually stick with wins.

The two methods in one paragraph each

Snowball: pay minimums on everything, then throw every spare dollar at the smallest balance. When it's gone, roll its payment into the next-smallest. You buy quick wins and motivation.

Avalanche: same rolling structure, but spare dollars go at the highest APR first. Mathematically optimal: every dollar lands where it stops the most interest.

A worked example (three debts, $300 extra per month)

  • Card A — $8,000 at 24% APR, $200 minimum
  • Card B — $3,500 at 19% APR, $90 minimum
  • Loan C — $12,000 at 7% APR, $250 minimum

Simulating month by month (interest accrues monthly, minimums paid on everything, the extra $300 rolls per each strategy):

  • Avalanche (Card A first): debt-free in 40 months, about $4,650 total interest.
  • Snowball (Card B first): debt-free in 41 months, about $5,160 total interest.

Avalanche saves roughly $510 and one month here. Real money — but spread over three and a half years. If crossing Card B off the list in year one keeps you paying the extra $300 at all, snowball wins in practice. Quitting costs more than either ordering.

When the gap gets big

The methods diverge when your APRs diverge. If your highest-rate debt is also your largest (a big 27% card next to a small 6% loan), avalanche's edge can grow to thousands. If your rates are all within a few points of each other, the ordering barely matters — pick by feel.

Modeling it in Excel

You don't need a simulation for a good-enough answer. For each debt, NPER(APR/12, -payment, balance) returns the months to payoff at a given payment — try it with the minimum, then with the minimum plus your extra, and watch the payoff date move. Sort the sheet by balance for snowball or by APR for avalanche, and point the extra payment at the top row. Re-sort when a debt closes and roll its payment down.

Two flags worth building: a red highlight when a payment doesn't even cover monthly interest (balance is growing), and an estimated debt-free date per debt so progress is visible.
