Invoice payment terms explained: net 30 vs net 15 vs due on receipt

"Net 30" is the most common payment term in business — and the least examined. Most freelancers put it on their first invoice because it sounds professional, then spend week five wondering where the money is. Here's what each term actually commits you to, and how to pick one on purpose.

What each term means

TermWhat it saysWhat it means in practice
Due on receiptPay when the invoice arrivesUsually paid in a few days — by clients who were going to pay fast anyway. Gives slow payers no concrete date to miss, which matters if you ever add late fees.
Net 7 / Net 14 / Net 15Full amount due 7/14/15 days after the invoice dateShort enough to keep cash moving, long enough for a client to run one payment cycle. The sweet spot for most freelance work.
Net 30Full amount due 30 days after the invoice dateThe corporate default. Clients with approval workflows treat day 30 as the earliest pay date, not the deadline.
2/10 net 302% discount if paid within 10 days; full amount by day 30An early-payment bribe. Works on invoices big enough that 2% moves a finance department; on a $900 invoice, $18 rarely changes behavior.

Two details trip people up. The clock starts on the invoice date — not when the client opens the email, and not when the work finished. And a net term is a ceiling you offered, so nobody pays early: quote net 30 and you have, in effect, scheduled your own payment for day 30.

The math nobody does: day 30 is really day 45

Say you finish work the last week of June and invoice July 1 on net 30. Payment is "due" July 31. The client's accounts-payable run is weekly, so the payment enters the July 31 batch at best — if the approval landed in time. Add a weekend, plus bank processing, and the money arrives in the first week of August: five to six weeks after you finished the work, with the client never once being "late."

That gap is you financing your client's business, interest-free. It's also why invoices with shorter terms get paid roughly twice as fast — not because clients respect them more, but because the whole pipeline starts sooner. Small-business mentoring organizations like SCORE and the SBA commonly steer new freelancers toward net 15 for exactly this reason: professional enough to feel standard, short enough that a slow cycle still pays you inside a month.

Which term for which client

SituationTerm to useWhy
Small or one-off project (under ~$500)Due on receipt — or 100% up frontChasing a small balance costs more than it's worth; small jobs shouldn't have a tail.
New client, project workNet 14 or net 15 Concrete due date, fast cash, zero eyebrows raised. Pair it with a deposit — terms protect the back of a project, deposits the front.
Long project (a month or more)Same term, applied to each milestone invoiceSplit the price into staged payments so the unpaid middle never grows — the milestone schedule guide covers the standard splits.
Ongoing retainer, monthly billingNet 15, invoiced the same day each monthPredictable rhythm on both sides; the due date becomes routine.
Corporate client with a procurement processNet 30 (they'll tell you)Often contractual on their side and not winnable. Price the wait into your rate instead of fighting it.
Client with a history of paying lateNet 7, plus late feesShorten the runway and put teeth in the terms — or require more up front.

Make the term hold up

  • Put it in the contract before work starts. A payment term that first appears on the invoice is a suggestion; one the client agreed to in writing is a commitment. One line does it: "Invoices are payable net 15 from invoice date."
  • Write the actual date on the invoice. "Due: September 3, 2026" outperforms "Terms: net 15" — nobody has to do date arithmetic to know they're late. Best practice is both, and it's one of the fields on our what-goes-on-an-invoice checklist.
  • Invoice the day you deliver. Every day between finishing and invoicing is a day you added to your own net term.
  • Decide now what happens at due date + 1. A term with no follow-up is decorative. Have a calm reminder sequence ready so the first nudge goes out on schedule, not when you get frustrated.

Paste-ready wording

For your quote or contract: "Payment terms: 50% deposit on acceptance; balance invoiced on delivery, payable net 15 from invoice date. Overdue balances accrue 1.5% monthly interest."
For the invoice itself: "Terms: Net 15 — due September 3, 2026. Payment by bank transfer or card (details below)."

Adjust the numbers to your situation — the structure is the point: a date the client agreed to, a date printed on the invoice, and a stated consequence.
