How to write a freelance proposal (structure + the pricing math)

Most proposal advice is about persuasion. But proposals rarely lose on prose — they lose on vague scope and ambiguous pricing. This guide covers the standard structure quickly, then spends most of its time where proposals are actually won: how to build the number, how to present it, and which payment terms belong in the proposal itself.

The structure clients expect

Winning proposals across industries converge on the same seven sections, in the order a client actually asks the questions:

SectionIts one job
1. Project summaryRestate the client's problem in their own words — the goal, the pain, what success looks like to them
2. Scope of workWhat you will do — and, just as load-bearing, what is not included
3. DeliverablesThe concrete things they receive, named and countable
4. TimelineStart, milestones, end — tied to deliverables, not vibes
5. PricingThe number, what it includes, and the payment schedule (the rest of this guide)
6. TermsDeposit, payment terms, revision limits, what happens when scope changes
7. Next stepOne obvious action: "Reply to accept" or a signature line — never "let me know your thoughts"

Two notes on the top of the document. First, open with their problem, not your bio — pages of credentials before the proposed work is one of the most common ways proposals lose. Second, speed matters: a good-enough proposal sent within a day or two of the conversation routinely beats a perfect one sent next week, because you're answering while the problem is still loud in the client's head.

Build the number before you write a word

An ambiguous price kills more proposals than a high one. The fix is to build the quote from parts you can defend out loud:

  1. Estimate the hours honestly. List the scope items and estimate each one separately — a per-task estimate is more accurate than one number for the whole project, and the list doubles as your scope section.
  2. Multiply by your real rate. Not a guess — the rate that covers your costs, taxes, and non-billable time. If you've never computed it, start with how to set your freelance rate or the free Rate Calculator.
  3. Add contingency. Projects grow. 10–15% on a well-defined scope, 20% when requirements are fuzzy or the client is new. This isn't padding — it's the observed gap between estimated and actual hours on almost every project.
  4. Add a fixed-fee risk premium if the price is fixed. A fixed fee transfers estimation risk from the client to you, and that transfer has a price — typically another 10–20% over the hourly-built number. If the client won't wear the premium, quote hourly with a cap instead. (Choosing between the models is its own decision — see hourly vs day rate vs project pricing.)

Worked example: a $4,800 quote you can defend

LineMathAmount
Scoped tasks (design 12h, build 22h, revisions 6h, launch 2h) 42 h × $85/h$3,570
Contingency (defined scope, new client)+15% $536
Fixed-fee risk premium+15%$616
Fixed price quotedrounded $4,800

You don't show this table to the client — you show the fixed price and what it includes. The table is for you: if the client asks "can you do it for $4,000," you know immediately that the honest answers are "yes, minus the revision round and launch support" (scope down) or "yes, billed hourly at $85 with a cap" (risk back to them) — never just a discount. Cutting the number without cutting anything else tells the client the first price was decoration.

Presenting the price

  • One option, or three — never five. A single well-scoped price is fine. If you offer tiers, three is the ceiling (good / better / best), each tier a genuinely different scope — more than that creates decision paralysis, and proposals with pricing breakdowns close meaningfully better than bare one-line quotes.
  • Show what's included, per component. "Website redesign: $4,800" invites haggling; five scope lines with one total invites a decision.
  • Anchor on the outcome, then state the number. Scope and deliverables come before price in the document for a reason — by the time the client reads the figure, they should know exactly what it buys.
  • Never apologize for the rate. No "I'm flexible," no "happy to work something out" before anyone has objected. If you volunteer softness, the negotiation starts below your number.

Payment terms belong in the proposal

The proposal is where payment terms are agreed — the invoice just restates them. Three things to state explicitly in the terms section:

  • The deposit. 30–50% up front is standard; how much deposit to ask for covers picking the number and the wording.
  • The schedule. Anything beyond a few weeks gets milestone payments tied to named deliverables — the milestone payment schedule guide has the standard splits (50/25/25, 30/30/30/10) and when to use each.
  • The clock. Due-on-receipt or net-14 on each invoice, and a late-fee line if you use one — see payment terms explained. Terms stated in an accepted proposal are terms the client already agreed to; terms that first appear on an invoice are a surprise.

Common ways proposals lose

  • A single hourly rate with no scope or estimated total — the client can't budget "$85/hour, we'll see."
  • Scope described only by what's included, so every extra feels "in scope" to the client. State exclusions — they're also your first line of defense against scope creep.
  • Credentials before the client's problem. Two sentences of relevant proof, placed after the summary, is plenty.
  • No expiry. Quote prices "valid for 14 days" so an acceptance six months later doesn't resurrect old pricing.
  • No follow-up. One check-in after 3–5 business days is professional; silence after that is your answer.
