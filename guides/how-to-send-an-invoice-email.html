How to write an invoice email that gets paid (with templates)

The invoice email is the most-sent email in freelancing and one of the least thought-about. That's mostly fine — it should be short and boring — but a few details decide whether it gets processed the day it lands or sits unopened behind a vague subject line until you're writing a reminder instead.

The subject line does half the work

Your client's inbox is where your invoice competes for attention, and accounts-payable people search their inbox by invoice number. Put the essentials directly in the subject:

Invoice #2026-014 from [Your Business] — $1,250 due Sep 5

Number, sender, amount, due date. A subject like "Invoice" or "March work" forces the client to open the email to know what it is — and unopened emails don't enter payment runs. If they file or forward it, the subject keeps working on your behalf inside their process.

Attach a PDF — don't paste the invoice into the body

The email is the cover note; the invoice is the document. Send the invoice as a PDF attachment with a clean filename ("Invoice-2026-014-YourBusiness.pdf"), because that's the thing a client saves, prints, or uploads to their accounting software. An invoice pasted into the email body can't be filed, mangles in forwarding, and looks improvised. The attachment itself should carry everything on the invoice checklist — number, dates, itemized lines, total, payment instructions.

What goes in the body: five lines

  1. What this is: the project or period the invoice covers, in the client's language — "the March content package," "phase 2 of the site build."
  2. The amount. Repeat it in the body so nobody has to open the attachment to know the size of the ask.
  3. The due date — as a real date. "Due September 5" beats "net 15"; nobody does date arithmetic on your behalf. (Pick the term deliberately — the payment terms guide covers which one to quote.)
  4. How to pay. Name the method(s) and point at the details on the invoice; if there's a payment link, put it here too.
  5. One human sentence. A thank-you or a note about the work. One — not a paragraph.

Send it the day you deliver the work. Every day between finishing and invoicing is a day added to your payment terms, and the goodwill from a just-delivered project is the best payment accelerant there is.

Template 1: standard invoice, existing client

Subject: Invoice #2026-014 from Sano Design — $1,250 due Sep 5

Hi [name],

Invoice #2026-014 for the March content package is attached — $1,250, due September 5 (net 15). Payment by bank transfer or card; details are on the invoice.

Really pleased with how the launch pieces turned out — thanks as always for the quick turnarounds on feedback.

[Your name]

Template 2: first invoice for a new client

Subject: Invoice #2026-021 from Sano Design — $800 due Sep 12

Hi [name],

Great to wrap up the first project together. Invoice #2026-021 is attached — $800, due September 12 (net 15, as per the quote).

One first-invoice note: payment is by bank transfer to the account on the invoice. If your accounts team needs me set up as a vendor or wants a W-9, send me the forms and I'll turn them around same day.

Looking forward to the next phase.

[Your name]

That vendor-setup line saves the most common first-invoice delay: the client's finance process discovering it can't pay you yet.

Template 3: deposit or milestone invoice

Subject: Invoice #2026-018 from Sano Design — $2,000 booking deposit, due on receipt

Hi [name],

Thanks for the go-ahead. As per the quote, invoice #2026-018 for the 50% booking deposit is attached — $2,000, due on receipt. The project start date is reserved for you and work begins as soon as the deposit lands.

Excited to get started on this one.

[Your name]

The one structural difference: the email restates what the payment unlocks ("work begins when paid"), because that's the agreement doing the protecting. The same shape works for each stage of a milestone schedule — name the delivered milestone, attach the invoice for that stage, state what starts when it's paid. How much to ask for up front is covered in the deposit guide.

Mistakes that slow payment down

  • Apologizing for invoicing. "Sorry to bother you with this" reframes an agreed payment as a favor. You did the work; the invoice is the normal next step.
  • Burying the invoice in a status update. Send project news and the invoice as separate emails — an invoice attached to paragraph six of a recap gets read, filed under "project," and never forwarded to whoever pays.
  • No amount or date in subject or body. Every click between your email and the payment details is a place the process stalls.
  • Inventing a new format each time. Same subject pattern, same body shape, sequential numbers — clients pay familiar invoices faster, and you stop rewriting from scratch.

When the due date passes

A good invoice email sets up the follow-through: because the due date was stated plainly, the first nudge on day one overdue is a calm statement of fact, not an awkward surprise. Have the reminder sequence ready before you need it, and if slow payment is a pattern with a client, put late-fee terms on the next quote.
