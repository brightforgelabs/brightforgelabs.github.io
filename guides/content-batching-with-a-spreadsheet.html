Content batching with a spreadsheet

Content batching — making many posts in one sitting instead of one post per day — is the single highest-leverage habit for small accounts. Most guides then sell you a scheduler subscription. You don't need one to start: a spreadsheet runs the whole system.

Why batching works (it's not discipline)

Creating a post has a fixed startup cost: deciding what to say, finding the reference, getting into writing mode. Do that daily and you pay the startup cost 30 times a month. Batch eight posts in one session and you pay it once — the second post takes half the time of the first, and the fifth takes a quarter. Batching is just refusing to pay the same setup tax every day.

Batch by task, not by post

The common mistake is finishing post 1 completely, then post 2, then post 3. Faster: run every post through one stage at a time, like an assembly line.

  1. Pick (10 min): pull 8–12 ideas from your idea bank — don't invent from a blank page mid-session.
  2. Outline (20 min): one line per post: the point, the angle, the call-to-action if any.
  3. Draft (60–90 min): write them all, ugly first. No editing yet — editing mid-draft is where sessions die.
  4. Edit & slot (30 min): tighten each draft, then assign each one a date and platform on the calendar.

That's a month of twice-a-week posting from one honest afternoon.

The spreadsheet that runs it

Two tables carry the whole system:

  • Idea bank: title, angle, content pillar, platform. Captured any time, spent during batch sessions. A batch session without an idea bank starts from a blank page — which is the thing batching was supposed to kill.
  • Calendar: date, platform, title, status (idea → drafted → scheduled → posted). During the session you watch a column of "idea" rows turn into "drafted" rows — and any week, a glance shows what's slotted and what's overdue.

Keep the drafts themselves wherever you write comfortably; the spreadsheet tracks state, not prose.

Batching's failure mode: the stale batch

Batching a full month sometimes ships posts that feel dated by week four. The fix isn't abandoning batching — it's leaving 20–30% of your slots empty for reactive posts, and treating the batch as the floor, not the ceiling. Consistency comes from the batch; timeliness comes from the gaps.
