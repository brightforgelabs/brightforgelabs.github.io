Web design deposits & payment schedules: how much, and when

Website projects have a payment problem built in: the work ships in stages, the client's excitement peaks at the start, and the finished site is the one thing you can't repossess. The fix is the same as everywhere in freelancing — a deposit plus milestones — but web work has its own standard shape, and it's worth knowing.

The short answers

  • 25–50% up front is the standard deposit band for web work, same as freelancing generally.
  • Smaller sites (under about $5k): 50% is the norm — two or three payments, front-loaded.
  • Bigger builds: 25–33% down, with the rest spread across milestones so no single unpaid stretch gets dangerous.
  • Work starts when the deposit clears. Stated in the quote, this turns a slow deposit into a schedule item instead of an awkward conversation.

The web-build standard: 30 / 30 / 30 / 10

Longer website projects have a schedule shape of their own, because a site has natural, client-visible checkpoints that most freelance work doesn't. The classic split hangs a payment on each one:

PaymentDue atWhat it covers
30%Signing Books your calendar and funds discovery, sitemap, and early strategy — the unglamorous work clients undervalue until they've seen it.
30%Design approval Mockups signed off. This is the project's biggest fork — collecting here means revisions were paid for before the build starts on top of them.
30%Build on staging A working site the client can click through. The deliverable proves itself; the invoice attached to it is hard to dispute.
10%Launch Go-live, handover, credentials. Small on purpose — see below.

On a $6,000 site that's $1,800 · $1,800 · $1,800 · $600 — and at no point in the project are you more than $1,800 deep in unbilled work.

Why the last payment is small

The final invoice is the one that goes bad, because at handover your leverage is gone and "can we just tweak one thing first?" season begins. The 10% tail is the web-specific version of the 25% rule: keep the amount that's unpaid when the work is functionally done small enough that a slow approval is an annoyance, not a cash-flow crisis. A $600 tail on a $6,000 build keeps launch moving; a $3,000 tail invites a six-week "final review."

Hang payments on approvals, not dates

Every payment above is tied to a reviewable deliverable the client signs off — approved mockups, a staging link that works — never to a calendar date or a percent-complete estimate. Web projects stall for client-side reasons constantly: content arrives late, feedback sits in someone's inbox, the domain login is lost. If payments ride on dates, their delay becomes your unpaid limbo. If payments ride on approvals, the invoice fires the moment the checkpoint is reached — and the quote should say the next phase starts when it's paid. That same logic is why out-of-scope requests get their own change orders and their own line items, not a quiet burial in the launch payment.

The clause for your quote

State the schedule in the proposal, as how you work, not as a request:

Payment schedule: 30% on signing (work begins when paid) · 30% on design approval · 30% when the build is live on staging · 10% at launch. Each stage is invoiced with its own due date; the next stage begins when the current invoice is paid. Invoices unpaid 10 days past the due date accrue a late fee of 1.5% per month.
