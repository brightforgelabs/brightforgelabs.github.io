Client won't pay your invoice? What to do, step by step

You've sent the polite reminders and the firm one, and the invoice is still unpaid. This guide covers what comes next: the escalation path from a direct conversation to a formal demand letter, and how to decide — with actual numbers — whether small claims, a collection agency, or walking away is the right last step.

This is general business information, not legal advice. Small-claims limits, interest rules, and collection law vary by state and country — check your local rules or ask a lawyer before formal action.

First, rule out the fixable causes

Before treating this as refusal, spend ten minutes confirming it isn't a process failure — because most "non-payment" is:

  • The invoice has an error — wrong amount, PO number, or entity name. Anything wrong restarts many companies' payment clocks. Check it against the invoice checklist.
  • It went to the wrong person. Your day-to-day contact often isn't who pays. Ask directly: "Who should invoices go to, and can you confirm this one reached them?"
  • There's an unspoken dispute. If the client is unhappy with the work, they sometimes go silent instead of saying so. Ask the question plainly — a dispute you can discuss is progress compared to silence.

If you haven't yet run a proper reminder sequence — day 1, week 1, week 3, each one notch firmer — do that first: the reminder email guide has the templates. This guide is for what happens when that ladder has been climbed and nothing moved.

Step 1 (~day 30): stop work and change channel

Two things happen together once an invoice is a month overdue and reminders have failed:

Stop new work for the client. Every hour you add to an unpaid account increases what's at risk. Announce it as policy, not punishment: "Per my payment terms, work is paused until the account is current. Happy to resume the day payment lands."

Get off email. Emails are easy to ignore; a phone call is a different kind of event. Call your contact, stay friendly, and ask one concrete question: "What has to happen for invoice #1042 to be paid this week, and who does it?" Write down the answer, then confirm it back by email the same day — "As discussed, [name] will process payment by Friday" — so the commitment exists in writing.

If your contact is a dead end at a larger client, escalate inside the company: accounts payable first, then their manager or the owner. Keep every message factual and civil — assume it may one day be read by a judge, because if this goes to small claims, it will be.

Step 2 (~day 45): the final notice, on your own letterhead

If a phoned commitment came and went, send a final notice email. It differs from the earlier reminders in three ways: it states a hard date, it applies any late-fee terms your invoice or contract actually contains (you can't invent fees now that were never agreed), and it names what happens next.

Subject: Final notice — invoice #1042, $[amount], now 45 days past due

Hi [Name],

Invoice #1042 for [project], due [due date], remains unpaid after several reminders and our call on [date]. The balance including the contractual late fee is now $[amount].

If payment isn't received by [date, 7–10 days out], I'll begin formal collection, starting with a written demand letter. I'd much rather resolve this simply — if there is a problem I'm not aware of, tell me and we'll sort it out.

Regards,
[Your name]

Step 3 (~day 60): the demand letter

A demand letter is a short formal letter stating the debt, the history, a deadline, and your intent to pursue legal remedies. Courts in many places expect to see one before a small-claims filing, and it often works on its own — it signals that ignoring you has stopped being free. Send it as a PDF attachment and by postal mail (certified or tracked, where available), and keep copies.

FINAL DEMAND FOR PAYMENT

Date: [date]
To: [Client legal name and address]
From: [Your business name and address]
Re: Unpaid invoice #1042 — $[amount]

This letter is a formal demand for payment of invoice #1042, issued [invoice date] for [work performed], due [due date], now [N] days past due. Reminders were sent on [dates] and payment was promised on [date] in our call of [date].

The amount due, including contractual late fees, is $[amount].

If full payment is not received by [date, 10–14 days out], I intend to pursue the remedies available to me, including filing a claim in small claims court and reporting the debt to a collection agency, without further notice.

Payment can be made by [methods]. If you dispute any part of this invoice, respond in writing by the same date.

[Signature]
[Your name, business name]

Keep the tone flat and factual. A demand letter full of anger reads badly in court; one that reads like an accountant wrote it does its job.

Step 4: pick the endgame with a calculator, not a grudge

If the demand letter deadline passes, you have three realistic options, and the right one is mostly a function of the amount owed:

OptionTypical costMakes sense when
Small claims courtFiling fees roughly $30–$150, no lawyer needed in most US small-claims courts; your time for filing and a hearingThe debt is under your state's small-claims limit (commonly $5,000–$12,500) and you have a paper trail: agreement, delivered work, invoice, reminders
Collection agencyUsually a 25–50% cut of what they recover, paid on successYou'd rather lose a third of the invoice than any more of your time, or the client is far away
Write it offThe invoice amount — and nothing furtherThe recovery effort costs more than the debt (see the math below); ask a tax professional how a genuine bad debt is treated in your situation

The walk-away math: estimate the hours the next step will take (small claims realistically runs 10–20 hours across paperwork, filing, and a hearing) and multiply by your hourly rate. If that number exceeds the invoice — typical for invoices under a few hundred dollars — the rational move is to write it off, fire the client, and put the hours into paying work instead. Winning a judgment also isn't the same as being paid: courts award judgments, but collecting on one is your job, and a client who ignored invoices may ignore judgments too.

Make the next one impossible

Almost everything in this guide gets cheaper one step earlier. For future projects:

  • Take a deposit — a client who has paid once nearly always pays again, and the deposit caps your exposure.
  • Split large projects into milestone payments so no single unpaid invoice can sink the month.
  • Use shorter payment terms and a stated late-fee policy agreed before work starts.
  • Notice on day one, not day forty. The whole ladder starts with knowing the moment an invoice goes overdue.
