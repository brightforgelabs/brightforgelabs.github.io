Milestone payment schedules: how to split a freelance project

A deposit protects the start of a project. On anything longer than a few weeks, the dangerous part is the middle — the stretch where you've done a lot of work and been paid for little of it. Milestones exist to keep that stretch short.

The principle behind every good split

At any point in the project, the work you've delivered and the money you've received should be roughly in balance. When they drift apart, one side is financing the other: if you're weeks of work ahead of the payments, you're lending the client money; if they've paid far ahead of what you've shipped, they're carrying the risk instead. Every standard split below is just a different way of keeping that balance for a different shape of project.

The standard splits

SplitShapeTypical use
50 / 50deposit → delivery Short projects (up to ~3–4 weeks). The 50% deposit is standard for projects under about $10k.
50 / 25 / 25deposit → midpoint → delivery Front-loaded work — strategy, architecture, discovery-heavy development — where most of your effort lands early.
25 / 50 / 25kickoff → first working version → delivery Projects where the big lift is in the middle: a $4,000 web project might run $1,000 to book, $2,000 when the first functional version is approved, $1,000 at launch.
30 / 30 / 30 / 10four phases Longer builds (2–3+ months) with several reviewable stages — common in web and product work. The small tail keeps the final approval moving without leaving real money at risk.

New client or short timeline: lean toward the front-loaded end. Long project with an established client: a 30% first payment is fine, because the later milestones are doing the protecting.

The 25% rule

Whatever split you pick, check it against one number: how much is still unpaid when you hand over the finished work? Keep that final chunk at or under about 25% of the project price. The last invoice is the one clients slow-pay — the work is in their hands and your leverage is gone — so it should never be a quarter of your month's income sitting in someone else's approval queue.

The same worst-case math as a deposit, on a $6,000, three-month project:

ScheduleUnpaid at final deliveryIf the last invoice goes bad
50% deposit, 50% on delivery$3,000 You financed half the project
50 / 25 / 25$1,500Painful, survivable
30 / 30 / 30 / 10$600An annoyance, not a crisis

Tie milestones to deliverables, not dates

The single most common milestone mistake is invoicing on a calendar ("second payment due March 1") or on a feeling ("50% complete"). Both invite argument. A milestone should be a named, reviewable deliverable: "wireframes for all five pages delivered," "staging site live with checkout working," "final files handed over." The client can look at the thing and see that it exists — which makes the invoice attached to it very hard to dispute.

  • 2–4 milestones fits almost every freelance project. More than that and you're spending your time invoicing.
  • Each milestone gets its own invoice, with a real due date and your normal payment terms.
  • The deposit rule applies down the chain: the next phase starts when the current milestone invoice is paid. Stated up front, that turns a slow payment into the client's schedule problem instead of your cash-flow problem.

Paste-ready wording for the quote

Like a deposit, a milestone schedule works best stated as how you work, in the quote, before anyone's invested. A schedule block beats a negotiation:

Payment schedule: 50% to book the project (work begins when paid) · 25% on approval of [midpoint deliverable] · 25% on final delivery. Each stage is invoiced separately; the next stage begins when the current invoice is paid.

For a four-phase build:

Payment schedule: 30% to book · 30% on [deliverable 1] · 30% on [deliverable 2] · 10% on final handover. Invoices are due on receipt; the next phase starts on payment.

If the client pushes back

Large companies with procurement processes often can't pay anything before a delivered milestone. That's a process constraint, not a red flag — answer it by making the first milestone small and early ("project setup and discovery document, delivered and invoiced in week one") rather than by dropping protection. What is a red flag is a client who wants a long project with everything payable at the end — that's the exact exposure this whole page exists to prevent, and the deposit guide covers how to read it.

When a milestone payment goes overdue

Milestone invoices go overdue like any other. The playbook is the same: a calm reminder sequence, late-fee terms stated on the invoice itself — plus the one lever a single-invoice project doesn't have: the pause. You stop work at the end of the paid phase, politely and without drama, because that's what the quote said would happen.
