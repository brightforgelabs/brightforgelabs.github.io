Questions to ask a client before starting a freelance project

Most freelance disasters — scope creep, late payment, the project that dies in week three — are traceable to a question that never got asked at the start. The kickoff conversation is the one moment when asking is free: the client expects questions, answers are easy to get, and nothing has been promised yet. Here are nine questions worth asking every time, why each one matters, and exact wording for the ones that feel awkward.

Before the questions: know what they're for

You're not interviewing the client for a magazine. Every question here exists to produce one of three things: a scope you can price, a payment structure you can enforce, or an early exit from a project you shouldn't take. If an answer would change your price, your terms, or your yes — ask. If it wouldn't, skip it; a 40-question intake form is how projects stall before they start.

The project questions

1. "What does done look like?"

Not "what do you want" — what, concretely, exists when this project is finished, and how will you both know? "A five-page website" is a deliverable. "A better online presence" is a mood. If the client can't describe done, the project has no edges, and a project with no edges is scope creep with a start date. Write the answer down; it becomes the deliverables list in your proposal almost verbatim.

2. "Why now?"

The answer tells you the real deadline and the real stakes. "Our funding round closes in October" is a project with a hard date and budget behind it. "We've been meaning to get to this for a while" is a project that can be deprioritized the moment your invoice arrives. Both are workable — but the second one is where you insist on a deposit and milestone billing rather than payment at the end.

3. "Who decides, and who else has opinions?"

You need two names: the person who can say yes (approves the work, signs off, pays), and anyone whose feedback gets collected along the way. Revision rounds double when these are different people and you only planned for one. If the answer is "the team decides," price for committee review — more rounds, longer waits — or ask for a single consolidated feedback pass per round, in writing.

4. "What happens to this after I hand it over?"

Who maintains it, publishes it, or builds on it? The answer surfaces hidden deliverables — training, documentation, source files, logins — that clients assume are included and freelancers assume are extra. Settle it now and it's a line in the proposal; settle it at handover and it's an argument.

The money questions

5. "What budget range do you have in mind?"

Freelancers skip this one out of politeness and lose more hours to it than to any other. You are not being rude; you're checking whether you're both in the same store. Paste-ready wording:

"So I can propose something that actually fits: do you have a budget range in mind? Projects like this usually land somewhere between [$X] and [$Y] depending on scope, and it helps to know which end we're aiming at."

Naming your own range in the question does two jobs: it anchors the conversation at your real prices, and it lets a mismatched client say so in one reply instead of after you've written a proposal. If they won't give any range, propose the smallest sensible first phase and let the working relationship earn the rest.

6. "How does payment work on your side?"

Larger clients have accounts-payable processes that will happily stretch your "due on receipt" into 45 days — and you want to know that before setting payment terms, not while chasing the invoice. Ask: who receives the invoice, do they need a PO number, and what's their standard payment run? Then set terms that fit reality — and put a deposit in front of it regardless: for most projects that's 30–50% up front, and for anything longer than a few weeks, a milestone schedule so no single unpaid chunk gets dangerous. 7. "What happens if the project stops partway?"

Projects get cancelled for reasons that have nothing to do with you — reorgs, budget freezes, a co-founder leaving. Agree now that work completed is paid for (this is what the deposit and milestones enforce mechanically), and for bigger projects consider a kill fee: a named percentage owed if the client cancels mid-phase. It's much easier to agree to in the honeymoon week than after the funding falls through.

The working-relationship questions

8. "Have you worked with a freelancer before?"

Not small talk. A client who has done this before knows that revisions are finite, that you have other clients, and that invoices are due when they say they're due. A first-timer often doesn't — and it's cheaper to set those expectations in the kickoff than to enforce them in week four. If they've worked with freelancers and it ended badly, ask what went wrong: the answer tells you either what they need or what they do to freelancers.

9. "Where does feedback happen, and how fast can you turn it around?"

One channel (email, one shared doc — anywhere written), one consolidated voice (see question 3), and a stated turnaround. A client who takes two weeks to review each round doubles your timeline in a way that looks like your lateness from the outside. Put their review turnaround in the proposal timeline explicitly: "timeline assumes feedback within 3 business days per round."

The one-page kickoff sheet

Nine questions is a conversation, not an interrogation — most take one sentence to answer. Capture the answers in one page:

QuestionAnswer becomes
What does done look like?Deliverables list
Why now?Real deadline + billing structure
Who decides / who has opinions?Revision terms
What happens after handover?Included vs. extra
Budget range?Proposal price band
How does payment work?Terms + deposit + schedule
What if it stops partway?Kill fee / milestone protection
Worked with freelancers before?How much educating to do
Feedback channel + speed?Timeline assumptions

Those answers are 80% of the proposal — which is the point. A proposal written from a kickoff sheet quotes a scope the client already described, at a price they already gestured at, on terms they already understand. Those get accepted; proposals that guess get "let me think about it" (and then you're in follow-up territory).

After the yes: track what you agreed

The kickoff answers only protect you if they're visible in week four when the "quick extra thing" request arrives. Keep one row per project — scope, price, deposit paid, next milestone, feedback terms — somewhere you actually look.
