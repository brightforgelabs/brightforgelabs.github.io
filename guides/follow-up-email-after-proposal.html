Follow-up email after a proposal: timing, templates, and when to stop

You sent the proposal, the client sounded excited, and now: nothing. Silence after a proposal usually isn't rejection — it's a busy inbox, an internal approval you can't see, or a decision that simply hasn't been made yet. What loses the project is either never following up or following up badly. Here's a 3-touch sequence with timing, templates, and a defined stopping point, so chasing a proposal stops being a mood and starts being a system.

Prevent the silence in the proposal itself

The best follow-up is the one you arranged in advance. Two lines in the proposal do most of the work:

  • A validity date on the price. "This quote is valid for 21 days" (the reasoning is in quote vs estimate vs invoice). It gives the decision a deadline that isn't you being pushy — and gives your final email something real to say.
  • A named next step. End the proposal with "I'll check in Thursday if I haven't heard back — happy to walk through any of it on a quick call." Now your follow-up is you doing what you said, not you hovering.

The 3-touch sequence

Published advice on timing varies — some guides say follow up after 2–3 business days, others say a week — but it converges on the same shape: about three follow-ups over 2–4 weeks, each one short, each with exactly one question, and then a clean stop. A sequence that fits inside a 21-day validity window:

TouchWhenIts one job
1. Check-in2–3 business days after sendingSurface easy blockers: did it arrive, is anything unclear
2. Value-addAbout a week laterRe-open the conversation by being useful, not by "bumping"
3. Close the loopAs the validity date approachesEnd the chase professionally and leave the door open

If the client told you their timeline ("the board meets next month"), anchor the sequence to that instead — a follow-up before their stated decision date reads as not listening.

Touch 1 — the check-in (day 2–3)

Subject: Re: [Project] proposal

Hi [Name],

Just checking the proposal landed in the right inbox. Is there anything in it you'd like me to clarify or adjust?

If a call is easier, I have 15 minutes Thursday afternoon — would that work?

[You]

Why it works: it's three sentences, it assumes nothing about why they're quiet, and it ends with a question that can be answered in one line. "Let me know what you think" makes the client design the next step; "would Thursday work?" lets them just say yes.

Touch 2 — add something (about a week later)

The second email has to earn its place in the inbox. "Just bumping this" doesn't; something useful does — a relevant example of similar work, an answer to a question they raised on the call, or a smaller way to start:

Hi [Name],

While this was with you I put together [a one-page outline of how I'd approach the first phase / an example of a similar project's before- and-after / the answer to the analytics question you raised] — attached.

If the full scope is more than you want to take on right now, the [first milestone] works as a standalone starting point at [$X].

Is this still on your radar for [quarter/month]?

[You]

Offering a smaller first phase isn't discounting — the price per unit of work stays the same; only the commitment shrinks. If price is the silent objection, this is the email that finds out. (Structure that smaller phase like any milestone split.)

Touch 3 — close the loop (validity date approaching)

Hi [Name],

The proposal I sent on [date] is valid through [date] — after that I'll assume the timing isn't right and free up the slot I was holding.

No hard feelings either way — if the project comes back around later, I'd be glad to revisit it with fresh numbers.

[You]

This is the email the validity date was for. It isn't a pressure tactic you invented this morning — it's a term of the offer they've had since day one, restated politely. Close-the-loop emails get replies surprisingly often, because "reply or lose the option" is finally a decision with a deadline. And when it doesn't get a reply, it still worked: you're released from the chase.

Keep the pipeline visible, or you'll drop it

Almost nobody decides to skip a follow-up; the proposal just falls out of view under newer work. The fix is the same one that works for unpaid invoices: one row per open proposal — client, amount, date sent, next touch due — reviewed for two minutes every Monday. What not to do

  • Don't send content-free bumps. Five "just floating this to the top!" emails read as pressure. Three emails that each contain something — a question, a resource, a deadline — read as professional.
  • Don't multiply channels. An email follow-up plus a LinkedIn message plus a text in the same week feels like being surrounded. Pick the channel the client uses and stay in it.
  • Don't guilt-trip. "I've followed up several times now…" makes replying awkward, and awkward conversations get postponed — the opposite of what you want.
  • Don't keep the price alive forever. Honoring a six-month-old quote at last spring's rate is the cost of skipping the validity date.

After the stop

Mark the row closed and move on — the time you'd spend on touch four through nine converts better as outreach to new prospects. Two exceptions worth a calendar entry: a one-line note when the validity expires if they went quiet after saying yes verbally, and a fresh "is this on your radar for next quarter?" email a few months out — old proposals do resurrect when budgets reset.
